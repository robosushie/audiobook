Long ago, before the road had streetlights, a round lighthouse stood at the bend of the coast near a fishing town. Its beam was not famous. It was faithful. Every night it turned, and the boats knew the rocks by the way the light paused and went on.

This story is about the child who kept that pause alive. His name was Farhan. He was nine in the year the keeper, his grandfather, hurt his foot on the stair. The town still needed the lamp. Farhan had watched the lighting a hundred times: trim the wick, check the oil, wipe the glass in circles, never in lines, because lines leave roads for the dirt.

"You will not go up alone," his mother said.

"Then come to the first landing," Farhan said. "I know the rest."

They climbed together until the landing, where his mother sat with the spare oil like a guard who loved him. Farhan went on. The stair smelled of salt and iron. At the top the lamp waited, a big patient eye. His hands shook, then remembered. He lit it. The beam woke and began its slow walk across the water.

Down in the harbor, his uncle's boat flashed a small mirror once. That meant seen. Farhan flashed back by passing his hand through the beam, a blink the family used as a wave. His grandfather, resting below with his foot on a cushion, heard the machinery settle into its night voice and closed his eyes in relief.

For three nights Farhan kept the light. He learned that history is not only kings and dates. It is also a job done carefully when the usual person cannot. On the fourth night his grandfather climbed again, slow, proud, and a little jealous of the perfect circles on the glass.

"You left the lamp better than you found it," he said. "That is the whole of keeping."

Years later the oil lamp became a lens and a bulb, and later still a quiet automatic beam. The building remained, round and white, with a bench facing the water. Children on school trips heard Farhan, grown and gray at the edges, tell the story without making himself tall. He always ended the same way.
