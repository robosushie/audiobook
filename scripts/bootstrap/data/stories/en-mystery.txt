The red marble was the loudest quiet thing in class. It lived in a jar on the art shelf, and every Friday the children took turns holding it up to the window. On Monday it was gone. Only a round space in the dust remained, like a period at the end of a sentence nobody had written.

Kabir pushed his glasses up. He was eight, and he liked clues more than guesses. "We will not accuse," he told his friend Sana. "We will notice."

They made a list. The window was shut. The jar was not broken. A smear of blue paint marked the shelf, and a thread of yellow wool clung to the jar's lip. The class rabbit, Dumpling, had a yellow blanket. The blanket was in the reading corner, and one corner of it was damp.

"Dumpling does not collect marbles," Sana said. "But someone who feeds Dumpling might."

They followed the damp. It led to the sink, then to a trail of tiny wet prints toward the cloakroom. The prints stopped at a raincoat with a blue paint thumb on the pocket. The raincoat belonged to Mr. Idris, the art teacher, who had spent Sunday washing brushes and, he later admitted, thinking about lunch.

Kabir knocked on the art-room door. "We are looking, not blaming," he said, which was a sentence he had practiced.

Mr. Idris opened his desk. Inside, beside a sandwich, sat the red marble. He laughed in surprise. "I used it to weigh my sketches so the fan would not steal them. I meant to put it back before the bell. Then the bell rang, and the marble became a paperweight with a secret."

He carried it to the jar himself and told the class the truth. No one was in trouble. Kabir wrote the last clue in his notebook: busy people misplace bright things. Sana added: ask before you decide.
