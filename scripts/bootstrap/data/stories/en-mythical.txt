On the night the town clock forgot to strike, a bird the color of moonlight landed on Mira's windowsill. It was no bigger than a teacup. Its wings were pale, and its eyes were kind, the way a night lamp is kind. Mira sat up in bed. She was eight, and she had been listening to the rain think about stopping.

"Are you lost?" she whispered.

The bird tilted its head. A small silver feather drifted onto the blanket. When Mira touched it, she heard a sound like a story clearing its throat. The feather showed her a picture: a hill beyond the last streetlight, and a round stone that used to sing.

"I can help," Mira said. She put on her slippers and her blue cardigan. Her mother was asleep. The house was quiet. Mira left a note on the table that said, Gone to walk a moon bird home. Back before breakfast.

The bird hopped to her shoulder. It weighed almost nothing. Together they crossed the garden, where the wet grass held their steps for a moment and then let them go. The street was empty. A cat watched from a wall and decided this was not its business.

At the hill, the stone sat under a neem tree. It was smooth and gray, with a crack like a smile. Mira knelt. "Is this the one?" The bird sang one soft note. The stone did not answer.

Mira remembered what her grandmother said about old things. They wake when someone sits with them and does not rush. So Mira sat. She told the stone about her school, about the sums she liked and the ones she did not, about the mango pickle that was too loud for her tongue. The bird added a hum. The rain became a mist.

Then the stone answered. It was not a voice with words. It was a low, happy tone, the sound of a bowl when you tap it gently. Light moved through the crack. The bird lifted, circled once, and settled on the stone as if it had found its chair.

"You live here," Mira said. "You were only visiting."

The bird dropped a second feather into her palm. This one was warm. In it she saw the town clock remembering how to strike, and her mother finding the note and smiling instead of worrying. Mira stood. She bowed, because some meetings deserve a bow.
