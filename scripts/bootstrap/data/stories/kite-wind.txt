On the highest hill outside town, the wind had a habit of arriving early. It tugged at scarves, flipped the pages of unread books, and made the dry grass bow as if a king were passing. Arun stood at the top with a kite he had built from two sticks, a sheet of red paper, and a yellow tail his sister had tied in uneven bows.

The kite's name was Pepper, because it looked spicy against the sky. Arun was eight, and he had a plan. He would send Pepper higher than the water tower. He would not let the string burn his fingers. He would bring the kite home without a tear, which was the hardest part of the plan.

His friend Lila arrived with a tin of orange sweets and a serious face. "The wind is showing off," she said. "Hold the spool. I will run."

They had tried yesterday. Pepper had hopped twice and sat down in a bush, looking embarrassed. Today the wind felt different, wider, like a road. Lila ran. Arun let the string slip through his hands in little gifts. Pepper climbed. It dipped, thought about the bush, and then chose the sky.

"Left!" Lila shouted, though the kite could not hear her. Arun pulled gently. Pepper steadied. The yellow tail wrote a quick letter the clouds did not bother to read. From up there, the town was a handful of roofs and one shining canal. A goat on the path looked smaller than a sentence.

Arun laughed so hard he had to plant his feet. The string hummed. It was a thin music, the kind you feel more than you hear. "It wants to keep going," he said.

"Then let it," said Lila, "but keep the end. Adventures still need a way home."

They took turns. When Lila held the spool, she talked to Pepper as if it were a classmate who needed encouragement. When Arun held it, he watched the knot he had tied and hoped it was the proud kind of knot. A crow circled once, decided the kite was not food, and went back to being a crow.

Then the wind made a bigger decision. Pepper shot sideways. The spool spun. The string slid and warmed Arun's palm. He did not let go. He stepped, stumbled, and sat down in the grass with the sky still attached to his hand.
