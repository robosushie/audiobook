The robot arrived in a cardboard box that said HANDLE WITH CARE and also, in smaller letters, THIS ONE LIKES QUESTIONS. Anika opened it at the kitchen table while her father boiled eggs. She was nine. Inside sat a round helper the color of a clear morning, with a yellow antenna and two careful hands.

"Hello," said the robot. "I am Bit. I am here to learn a street. May I learn yours?"

Anika's street had a bakery, a noisy dog named Moti, and a lamp that blinked when it felt shy. "You may," she said. "Do not scare Moti. He is loud because he is small."

Bit rolled beside her. Its wheels were quiet. At the bakery it asked why bread smells like a hug. Anika said the baker, Mrs. Rao, woke early and talked to the dough. Bit stored that under Important. At the corner it measured the blinking lamp and wrote a note: lamp needs a friend, not a scolding.

They found the real problem at the park gate. The gate's little solar panel was covered with a fallen poster. The panel was supposed to charge the path lights. Without it, the path would be dark for the evening walkers.

"I can lift the poster," Bit said. "I would like a human to decide. I am strong, and strength should ask."

"Lift it," said Anika. "The path belongs to everyone."

Bit peeled the poster away and folded it neatly. Sun touched the panel. A tiny green light woke up. Bit's antenna glowed the same green, pleased to match.

Moti arrived, barked twice, and sniffed Bit's wheel. Bit offered a recorded purr. Moti sat down, which for Moti was a speech of welcome.

That night the path lights came on, one by one, like sentences finishing themselves. Neighbors walked. Mrs. Rao brought buns. Anika's father said the eggs had been the easy part. Bit stood by the gate and answered questions: what time the lights would sleep, whether robots eat buns (they do not), and how to say thank you to a machine (the same way you say it to anyone).

Before the box was packed again, Anika asked, "Will you forget our street?"

"I keep what I am told to keep," Bit said. "Please tell me to keep the shy lamp, the loud dog, and the girl who let me help."

"Keep them," Anika said.

Bit touched its antenna to her palm, a light tap, warm as a mug. Then it folded into the box, still itself, already full of one small map. Anika wrote on the cardboard, Learned our street. Left the lights on. She listened to the path humming softly, a future that had decided to be kind.

A week later the lamp at the corner stopped blinking. Anika checked the poster site first, then the loose wire the way Bit had shown her, with the power off and a grown-up watching. Her father tightened one screw. The lamp shone steadily, shy no longer. Anika said, "It needed a friend," and her father, who did not usually talk to lamps, said, "Then we were the friends."

She wrote Bit a letter on paper, because robots that leave can still be addressed. Thank you for asking before you lifted. The dog likes you. Please keep our street as a place, not a prize. She gave the letter to Mrs. Rao, who knew someone who knew the workshop. Maybe it would arrive. Maybe the keeping was already done.

At night the path lights came on in order, like a sentence you can trust. Moti barked once, a small bark, just to be counted. Anika stood at the gate and did not feel the future as a machine coming closer. She felt it as a light that stays on because somebody noticed a poster, a panel, and a question worth asking.
