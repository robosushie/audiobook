When Leela was six, the empty lot behind her building was a place of dust and bottle caps. When she was ten, it had twelve young trees and a bench someone had painted green. This is the true-feeling story of how a child planted a small forest and taught a street to wait for shade.

Leela lived with her aunt, who worked at the clinic and came home with tired feet and sharp jokes. One hot April, Leela carried a sapling home from a school drive. It was a neem, thin as a question. "Where will it live?" her aunt asked.

"In the lot," Leela said. "If we ask."

They asked the neighbors. Mr. Joseph said yes if no one played cricket into his windows. Mrs. Fernandes said yes if there was a path for her morning walk. The children said yes immediately, because yes is a kind of running. Together they cleared bottle caps into a sack. Leela dug the first hole with a spoon until a uncle with a real shovel took pity and joined.

She watered at dusk, when the sun was no longer arguing. She named the neem Steady. When a goat wandered in, she built a ring of stones. When rain forgot the month, she carried a bucket until her arms hummed. Other children brought seeds in folded paper: hibiscus, a curry leaf, a tree nobody could identify that they called Surprise.

Not every plant stayed. Two dried up. Leela cried once, behind the stairwell, then wrote their names in a notebook so the trying would not disappear. Her aunt read the names and said, "A gardener is a person who returns."

By the second year, Steady was taller than Leela's shoulder. Birds tested the branches and held meetings. The bench appeared after Leela wrote a letter to the ward office with very neat handwriting and a drawing of shade. The office sent paint. The neighbors sent time.

People began to call her the lot's gardener, which made her shy and proud at the same time. She still did her homework on the steps. She still forgot her water bottle. She was not a statue of a hero. She was a girl who kept a promise to a thin tree.
